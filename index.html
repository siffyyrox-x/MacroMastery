Measuring GDP and Economic Growth

Fast: 1.5 hSteady: 3 h Pure calculation unit. No graphs are examined here. Drill the arithmetic until it is automatic.

The idea in one breath

GDP is the price tag on everything a country finished making inside its borders this year. Three rules keep you out of trouble: count it only if it is final (not an ingredient), only if it was made here (not imported), and only if it was made this year (not resold).

The two ways to measure it

Every taka someone spends is a taka someone else earns. So spending and income must give the same total. That is why both approaches land on the same number, and why you can use one to check the other in an exam.

Expenditure approachGDP = C + I + G + (X − M) C consumption · I investment · G government purchases · X exports · M imports Income approach
GDP = Wages + Rent + Interest + Profit + Indirect taxes − Subsidies + Depreciation

The four traps that cost marks

  • Intermediate goods. Flour used to bake bread is not counted. Only the bread.
  • Used goods. A second-hand car was already counted when new.
  • Transfer payments. Pensions and unemployment benefits buy nothing, so they are not in G.
  • Financial trades. Buying shares or bonds is not production.

Nominal vs Real GDP, and the deflator

Nominal GDP rises when prices rise even if the country makes nothing extra. Real GDP strips prices out by valuing every year's output at one fixed base year price. The gap between the two is pure inflation, and the deflator measures that gap.

Nominal GDP = current quantity × current price Real GDP = current quantity × base-year price GDP deflator = (Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP) × 100

Note the shortcut you will need: in the base year, nominal GDP and real GDP are the same number, so the deflator is always exactly 100 in the base year. If you compute a base-year deflator that is not 100, you have made an arithmetic error.

Worked example

ItemYr 1 qtyYr 1 priceYr 2 qtyYr 2 price
Rice100Tk 50120Tk 60
Fish50Tk 20060Tk 250
Nominal Yr 2 = (120×60) + (60×250) = 7,200 + 15,000 = Tk 22,200
Real Yr 2 (Yr 1 base prices) = (120×50) + (60×200) = 6,000 + 12,000 = Tk 18,000
Deflator Yr 2 = (22,200 ÷ 18,000) × 100 = 123.33 → prices rose 23.33%

The circular flow

Money goes round between four sectors: households, firms, government and the rest of the world. Households supply labour, land and capital through factor markets and earn income. They spend it in goods markets as consumption (C). Firms add investment (I), government adds G, and the rest of the world adds net exports (X − M). Because every taka spent is a taka earned, GDP can be measured three ways and all three must agree: output, income, or expenditure.

ApproachAdd upNever include
ExpenditureC + I + G + (X − M) Intermediate goods · used goods · financial transactions (shares, bonds) · transfer payments (pensions, benefits) · non-market activity (home gardening, unpaid housework) · illegal or unreported work
IncomeWages + rent + interest + profit + mixed income + taxes on production − subsidies + depreciationTransfer payments · capital gains · private gifts · income earned abroad · sale of used goods

Watch the two odd ones in the income approach

Taxes on production are added because they are part of the final price buyers actually pay. Subsidies are subtracted because they are government money, not earnings from production.

Uses and limitations of Real GDP

Real GDP measures production, not wellbeing. Six things it misses:

  • Household production — cooking, cleaning, childcare at home are unpaid, so uncounted.
  • Underground activity — cash-in-hand work and black markets go unrecorded.
  • Political freedom and social justice — high GDP says nothing about rights or fairness.
  • Health and life expectancy — a rich country can still have poor healthcare.
  • Leisure time — GDP rises when people work longer, but rest has value too.
  • Environmental quality — pollution boosts GDP short term and costs welfare long term.
Practice · short answer

A household hires a cleaner for Tk 5,000 a month. The following year the cleaner marries into the household and does the same work unpaid. What happens to GDP, and what does this reveal about GDP as a welfare measure?

GDP falls by Tk 60,000 a year, even though exactly the same work is being done and the household is no worse off. Paid cleaning is a market transaction and counts; household production is not counted because it is never bought or sold.

This exposes the household production limitation: GDP measures marketed output, not welfare. Countries where more work happens inside the home will have GDP understated relative to countries where the same work is bought in, which makes raw GDP comparisons across countries misleading.

Practice · short answer

Classify each for the expenditure approach: (i) flour bought by a bakery, (ii) a second-hand motorcycle, (iii) a government pension payment, (iv) a new house bought by a household, (v) shares in a company.

(i) Excluded — intermediate good, its value is already inside the price of the bread.
(ii) Excluded — used good, counted when first produced.
(iii) Excluded — transfer payment, no good or service is produced in exchange.
(iv) Included as Investment (I) — this is the exception to remember: new housing is I, not C.
(v) Excluded — a purely financial transaction, nothing is produced.

Monitoring Jobs and Inflation

Fast: 1.5 hSteady: 3 h Calculation unit again. Definitions matter as much as arithmetic here, because sorting people into the right box is the question.

The idea in one breath

Split the country into boxes. Everyone 16+ who is not in jail or hospital is the working-age population. Of those, anyone working or actively hunting is in the labour force. Every rate you will ever be asked for is just one box divided by another. Get the boxes right and the maths is trivial.

The boxes

Labour force = Employed + Unemployed Unemployment rate = (Unemployed ÷ Labour force) × 100 Labour force participation rate = (Labour force ÷ Working-age population) × 100 Employment-to-population ratio = (Employed ÷ Working-age population) × 100

Read the denominator twice

Unemployment rate divides by the labour force. Participation rate divides by the working-age population. Swapping these two denominators is the most frequent lost mark in this whole course. The working-age population is always the bigger number.

The four types of unemployment

TypeWhat causes itExam giveaway phrase
FrictionalNormal job search; people moving between jobs "recent graduates searching", "voluntarily quit", "between jobs"
StructuralSkills no longer match the jobs that exist "automation", "technological change", "factory closed permanently"
CyclicalThe business cycle; a recession "recession", "downturn", "slump in demand"
NaturalFrictional + Structural (cyclical is excluded) this is what remains at full employment
Natural unemployment = Frictional + Structural Full employment ⟺ actual unemployment rate = natural rate Output gap = Real GDP − Potential GDP

The link you must be able to state: if actual unemployment sits above the natural rate, there is cyclical unemployment, real GDP is below potential GDP, and the output gap is negative (a recessionary gap). If actual sits below natural, the gap is positive (an inflationary gap). At full employment the gap is exactly zero.

CPI and the inflation rate

The CPI prices a fixed basket. You keep base-year quantities frozen and only let prices move. If an exam table gives you current-year quantities as well, they are a deliberate distractor.

CPI = (Cost of basket at current prices ÷ Cost of same basket at base prices) × 100 Inflation rate = ((CPI this year − CPI last year) ÷ CPI last year) × 100

CPI vs GDP deflator: the comparison question

This is asked almost every year, so learn one clean reason rather than four vague ones.

CPIGDP deflator
CoversConsumer goods onlyAll domestic production: C, I, G and exports
ImportsIncluded (consumers buy them)Excluded (not made here)
ExportsExcludedIncluded
BasketFixed base-year quantitiesCurrent-year quantities

Best single sentence to write: "The CPI includes imported goods but excludes exports and capital goods, while the GDP deflator does the reverse, so when import prices rise faster than export prices the CPI shows higher inflation than the deflator."

The four CPI biases (all push the same way, so CPI overstates true inflation): new goods bias, quality change bias, commodity substitution bias, outlet substitution bias.

Why unemployment is a problem

Lost incomes and production. Benefits never fully replace lost wages, and less production means less consumption and investment, lowering living standards now and later.
Lost human capital. Long spells out of work permanently damage a career — a manager forced into a low-skill job may never return to the same level. The burden is not shared equally, which makes unemployment a political problem as well as an economic one.

Who counts as unemployed

You must be available for work and in one of three categories:

  1. Without work but made specific efforts to find a job in the previous four weeks.
  2. Waiting to be called back to a job you were laid off from.
  3. Waiting to start a new job within 30 days.

Two groups the official rate leaves out

Marginally attached workers want work and have looked recently, but are not searching right now. If they stopped because they kept failing, they are discouraged workers.
Economic part-time workers want full-time jobs but cannot find one, so they are only partly employed.
Both are excluded from the official unemployment rate, which therefore understates labour underuse.

Most costly unemployment is long-term job loss. Short spells and voluntary job changes cost far less, but the headline rate hides the difference, so the same number can mean very different things.

What raises the natural rate

InfluenceEffect
Age distributionA young population means more first-time seekers and more job switching, so frictional unemployment is higher.
Pace of structural changeRapid technological change or intense global competition raises structural unemployment; stable technology keeps it low.
Minimum wageSet above the market-clearing wage it creates a surplus of labour, especially among low-skilled and entry-level workers.
Efficiency wageFirms pay above market rate to raise productivity and cut turnover; fewer workers are hired than at the lower wage.
Unemployment benefitsMore generous benefits reduce the urgency to take a job, lengthening search. Europe's higher natural rates versus the US are the standard example.

The common thread

Minimum wage and efficiency wage work the same way: a real wage above equilibrium means firms hire fewer workers, so the natural rate rises.

Why inflation and deflation are problems

  • Redistributes income. With long-term wage contracts, unexpected inflation raises prices before wages, so workers lose purchasing power and employers gain. Deflation reverses it.
  • Redistributes wealth. Loans are fixed in money terms. Unexpected inflation means the money repaid buys less, so borrowers gain and lenders lose. Unexpected deflation does the opposite.
  • Lowers real GDP and employment. An unexpected inflation boom is short-lived; spending drops, real GDP falls below potential and unemployment rises. Unexpected deflation is worse still.
  • Diverts resources from production. People stop doing their jobs and start forecasting prices. Zimbabwe's hyperinflation is the extreme case.

High inflation is not the same as a high price level

High inflation means prices are rising fast right now. A high price level means prices are already high after years of past inflation. A country can have a very high price level and near-zero inflation.
Practice · short answer

A country introduces far more generous unemployment benefits and raises the minimum wage well above the market-clearing level. Explain the effect on the natural rate of unemployment and on full employment.

Both measures raise the natural rate.

The minimum wage above equilibrium means the real wage is above the market-clearing level, so the quantity of labour demanded falls short of the quantity supplied. That surplus is unemployment, concentrated among low-skilled and entry-level workers, and it is structural.

More generous unemployment benefits reduce the incentive to find work quickly, so people search longer before accepting a job. That lengthens spells and raises frictional unemployment.

Consequence: since natural unemployment = frictional + structural, the natural rate rises on both counts. Because full employment is defined as the actual rate equalling the natural rate, full employment now occurs at a higher measured unemployment rate, and potential GDP is correspondingly lower.

Practice · short answer

Inflation turns out much higher than anyone expected. Identify who gains and who loses among (i) a worker on a three-year fixed wage contract, (ii) a bank that issued a 10-year fixed-rate mortgage, (iii) that mortgage borrower.

(i) The worker loses. Prices rise but the money wage is locked, so the real wage falls and purchasing power is lost. The employer gains, because revenue rises while the wage bill does not — this is inflation redistributing income.

(ii) The bank loses. Repayments are fixed in money terms, so the money it gets back buys less than expected. The fixed interest rate does not cover the loss in real value.

(iii) The borrower gains. They repay in money worth less than the money they borrowed — inflation redistributing wealth from lender to borrower.

The general rule: unexpected inflation moves value from lenders to borrowers, and unexpected deflation moves it back.

Graph Bootcamp: from a blank page to a full answer

Fast: 1 hSteady: 2 h Do not skip this. Every remaining unit uses the same three skills. Learn them once here and Units 3 to 6 become mostly bookkeeping.

The whole secret, up front

All three models in this course are the same picture with different labels. Two lines cross. Something happens. One line moves. They cross somewhere new. You describe what changed. That is it. If you can do that once, you can do it for AD–AS, loanable funds and the money market without learning anything genuinely new.

Step 1 · What the two axes mean

Every graph has a vertical axis (up the side) and a horizontal axis (along the bottom). In economics the rule never changes:

Vertical axis = the PRICE of the thing Horizontal axis = the QUANTITY of the thing

Once you know what "the thing" is, you know both axis labels. Memorise this one table and you can label any diagram in this course correctly:

ModelThe "thing" being tradedVertical axis = its priceHorizontal axis = its quantity
AD–ASAll goods and servicesPrice levelReal GDP
Loanable fundsBorrowed moneyReal interest rateLoanable funds
Money marketMoney itselfNominal interest rateReal money (M/P)

Why the interest rate is a "price": it is what you pay to borrow money, or what you give up by holding money instead of lending it. So it belongs on the vertical axis exactly like any other price.

Step 2 · Reading a single point

A point on a graph is one pair of facts. Drop a dashed line down to find its quantity, and a dashed line across to find its price. That is all a point ever means.

Step 3 · Why lines slope the way they do

You only ever need two shapes, plus one special vertical line. Learn the reason, not the picture, because the reason is what earns the marks.

ShapeWhich curvesThe one-line reason

Upward ╱

Memory hook

Buyers slope down, sellers slope up, and anything the price cannot influence stands straight up. There are only two vertical curves in the whole course: LAS and MS.

Step 4 · Equilibrium is just the crossing point

Where the two lines cross, the amount buyers want equals the amount sellers offer, so nothing pushes the price any further. Mark it with a dot, call it E₀, and drop your two dashed lines to read off P₀ and Q₀. Every question in this course is: the crossing point moved — where to, and what does that mean?

Step 5 · The one distinction that decides your grade

Movement ALONG a curve vs a SHIFT OF the curve

This single confusion costs more marks in ECO 102 than anything else. If the price on the vertical axis changes, you slide along the existing curve. The curve does not move. Anything else that matters moves the whole curve sideways.

Say it correctly in the exam. The wording is marked:

SituationCorrect phraseWrong phrase
Interest rate rose, so firms borrow less "the quantity of loanable funds demanded fell" "demand fell"
Firms expect higher profits, so they borrow more "demand increased, DLF shifted right" "quantity demanded rose"

Step 6 · Which way does it move?

Shifts are always sideways, left or right. Never up or down, and never rotate a curve. Use this test, which works for every curve in the course:

MORE of it at every price → shift RIGHT → LESS of it at every price → shift LEFT ←

Ask yourself one question: at the same price as before, is there now more or less? If less, the line moves left. A left shift of an upward curve looks like it went "up", and that is fine, but always think of it as horizontal or you will misread the new quantity.

Step 7 · Reading the new equilibrium in three moves

Once one curve has moved, find the answer mechanically. Do not guess.

  1. Find the new crossing point and mark it E₁.
  2. Compare heights. Is E₁ higher or lower than E₀? Higher means the price rose.
  3. Compare sideways positions. Is E₁ left or right of E₀? Right means the quantity rose.

The reliable shortcut

When only one curve moves, the new equilibrium slides along the curve that stayed still. So if AD stays put and SAS shifts left, E₁ must sit further up the unchanged AD curve, which immediately tells you price up and quantity down. You can get the answer before you have drawn anything neatly.

Step 8 · The universal four-step recipe

Use this exact sequence for every diagram question in the exam:

Aggregate Demand and Aggregate Supply

Fast: 2.5 hSteady: 5 h The most important unit in the course. Graphs start here and Section B leans on this heavily. Do not move on until you can draw all three curves from memory.

The idea in one breath

This is supply and demand for the whole economy at once. The quantity on the horizontal axis is real GDP. The price on the vertical axis is the price level. Everything you already know about shifting curves still works; only the labels change.

The three curves

CurveShapeWhy
LAS
Long-run aggregate supply		Vertical at potential GDP Money wages fully adjust, so the real wage returns to full employment no matter what the price level is. Potential GDP depends on resources and technology, not on prices.
SAS
Short-run aggregate supply		Upward sloping The money wage rate is fixed in the short run. A higher price level with fixed wages means a lower real wage, so firms hire more and produce more.
AD
Aggregate demand		Downward sloping Wealth effect, intertemporal substitution and international substitution (below).

Why AD slopes down — the three effects

  • Wealth effect. A higher price level shrinks the real value of savings, so people consume less.
  • Intertemporal substitution. A higher price level raises interest rates, so people delay buying cars, houses and machinery.
  • International substitution. A higher domestic price level makes local goods dear relative to foreign ones, so exports fall and imports rise.

What shifts what

Shifts ADShifts SASShifts LAS
Expected future income, inflation, profits · Fiscal policy (G, taxes, transfers) · Monetary policy (money supply, interest rate) · World economy (exchange rate, foreign income) Money wage rate · Prices of other inputs, e.g. oil · Change in potential GDP Quantity of labour · Quantity of capital · Technology

Oil is a SAS shifter, never an AD shifter

An oil price rise raises firms' costs, so it moves SAS to the left. It does not touch LAS, because potential GDP depends on resources and technology, not on input prices. Writing "oil prices reduce aggregate demand" is a serious conceptual error.

The oil shock, step by step

Stagflation, and why it is unusual

Normally when output falls, prices fall too, because a slump is caused by weak demand. An oil shock is different: it hits costs, so output falls and prices rise at the same time. That combination has its own name, stagflation, and its presence is the fingerprint of a supply shock rather than a demand shock.

The three possible equilibria

SituationConditionGap nameUnemployment
Above full employmentReal GDP > Potential GDPInflationary gapBelow natural rate
Full employmentReal GDP = Potential GDPNo gapEquals natural rate
Below full employmentReal GDP < Potential GDPRecessionary gapAbove natural rate

How the economy fixes itself (the money wage rate)

This is the part students skip, and it is worth marks on its own.

  • Recessionary gap. Unemployment is above the natural rate, so there is a surplus of workers. That pushes the money wage rate down. Lower wages cut firms' costs, so SAS shifts back right until real GDP returns to potential. The price level ends lower.
  • Inflationary gap. Unemployment is below the natural rate, so workers are scarce and the money wage rate is bid up. Higher costs shift SAS left until real GDP falls back to potential. The price level ends higher.

Either way the economy returns to LAS. The adjustment is slow, which is why governments intervene.

Exam-style practice · 10 marks

A severe drought sharply raises food and raw-material prices across an economy that began at full employment.

(a) Use the AD–AS model to explain the effect on real GDP, the price level and unemployment in the short run. Draw a labelled diagram. (6)
(b) Identify the output gap and explain two ways the economy could return to potential GDP. (4)

Step 1 — the diagram

Draw exactly Fig. 2 above, relabelled: AD unchanged, SAS₀ shifting left to SAS₁, LAS vertical and unmoved, E₀ on LAS, E₁ up and to the left of it. Label P₀, P₁, Y₀, Y₁ and mark the recessionary gap on the horizontal axis.

SAS shifts left. E₀→E₁ climbs the unchanged AD curve, so the price level rises P₀→P₁ while real GDP falls Y₀→Y₁, opening a recessionary gap to the left of LAS.

Step 2 — which curve shifts and why

The drought raises the price of raw materials, which is an input cost for firms. At every price level firms are now willing to supply less output, so short-run aggregate supply shifts left, SAS₀ → SAS₁. Aggregate demand is unchanged, because nothing has happened to wealth, expectations, fiscal or monetary policy, or the world economy. LAS is unchanged, because potential GDP depends on the quantity of labour and capital and on technology, none of which the drought has permanently altered.

Step 3 — the new equilibrium

Equilibrium moves from E₀ to E₁ along the unchanged AD curve. The price level rises from P₀ to P₁ and real GDP falls from Y₀ to Y₁. Since firms produce less they employ fewer workers, so unemployment rises above the natural rate. Rising prices together with falling output is stagflation.

Step 4 — consequences and part (b)

The output gap is negative: real GDP is below potential GDP, so this is a recessionary gap. It is a problem because output and income are permanently lost, unemployment above the natural rate destroys human capital as skills decay, and household living standards fall.

Two routes back to potential:
1. Automatic wage adjustment. Unemployment above the natural rate creates a surplus of labour, which pushes the money wage rate down. Firms' costs fall, SAS shifts back right to SAS₀, real GDP returns to potential and the price level falls back. This works but is slow.
2. Expansionary policy. The government raises spending or cuts taxes, or the central bank increases the money supply and lowers the interest rate. Either shifts AD right, restoring real GDP to potential faster. The trade-off is that this raises the price level further rather than reversing the inflation.

Also examinable from Unit 04

Economic growth and inflation in the AS–AD model

Put the two long-run forces on one diagram and the whole model clicks.

  • Economic growth comes from a growing labour force and rising labour productivity. These raise potential GDP, so LAS shifts right.
  • Inflation comes from aggregate demand growing faster than potential GDP. AD shifts right by more than LAS, so the price level rises.
  • Growth without inflation happens when AD grows at exactly the same rate as LAS. Real GDP rises and the price level is stable.

The sentence to remember

Inflation is not caused by growth. It is caused by demand growing faster than capacity.

Don't confuse the two curves

Real-world growth and inflation are not smooth. Real GDP fluctuates around potential GDP, and those fluctuations are the business cycle. In business-cycle questions we study the fluctuations; in growth questions we study the trend in LAS.
Practice · short answer

An economy\u2019s potential GDP grows by 3% a year while aggregate demand grows by 7% a year. Using AS\u2013AD, explain the outcome. What if AD instead grew by exactly 3%?

AD growing at 7% against LAS at 3%. LAS shifts right by 3%, so capacity genuinely rises and real GDP grows. But AD shifts right by more than LAS, so the extra demand cannot be met by extra capacity alone and is absorbed by rising prices. Result: real GDP grows, and the price level rises persistently — this is demand-pull inflation. If AD keeps outpacing LAS, real GDP is pushed above potential and an inflationary gap opens, with unemployment below the natural rate.

If AD grew at exactly 3%. AD and LAS shift right by the same amount, so the intersection moves horizontally. Real GDP grows at 3% with a stable price level — growth without inflation. This is the ideal outcome and shows the point: inflation comes from the gap between demand growth and capacity growth, not from growth itself.

Finance, Saving and the Loanable Funds Market

Fast: 2 hSteady: 4 h High-yield unit. Budget deficits, crowding out and Ricardo-Barro are near-certain Section B material.

The idea in one breath

Bundle every bank, bond and share market in the country into one giant market for borrowing. Savers supply the funds, investors and the government demand them, and the price that clears the market is the real interest rate.

Where the funds come from

Household income splits three ways, and total spending splits four ways. Setting them equal gives the identity the whole chapter rests on:

Y = C + S + T   (what income is used for) Y = C + I + G + X − M   (what is spent) ⟹ I = S + (T − G) + (M − X) private saving + government saving + foreign borrowing National saving = S + (T − G)

The two curves

CurveSlope and reasonShifts when…
DLF demand Downward. A higher real interest rate makes fewer investment projects profitable, so firms borrow less. Expected profit changes · a government budget deficit appears or grows
SLF supply Upward. A higher real interest rate rewards saving, so households supply more funds. Disposable income · expected future income · wealth · default risk

Movement along vs shift of the curve

A change in the real interest rate moves you along a curve. Anything else shifts the curve. Examiners test this deliberately, so never write "the interest rate rose so demand fell"; write "the quantity of loanable funds demanded fell".

Government in the market

Crowding out, explained simply

The government and private firms are queuing at the same window for the same pool of savings. When the government borrows more, it bids the interest rate up, and some firms that would have invested at the old rate now walk away. Government borrowing has "crowded out" private investment.

An important detail worth a mark: investment falls by less than the size of the deficit, because the higher interest rate also persuades households to save more, which partly refills the pool.

The Ricardo-Barro effect

This is the counter-argument, and questions often ask for it explicitly.

The logic: taxpayers are rational. They know a deficit today means higher taxes tomorrow. So they save more now to pay those future taxes. That extra private saving shifts SLF right by exactly the same amount that the deficit shifted DLF right.

The result: the real interest rate is unchanged, investment is unchanged, and there is no crowding out. Only the quantity of funds traded rises. Parkin treats this as an extreme case, with reality probably somewhere in between.

Ricardo-Barro: both curves shift right by the same distance, so the real interest rate is unchanged and investment is unaffected. Only the quantity traded rises, so there is no crowding out.

The real interest rate is unchanged, investment is unchanged, and there is no crowding out. Only the quantity of loanable funds traded increases. Parkin regards this as an extreme case, though some partial adjustment of this kind probably does occur.

The real interest rate

Real interest rate ≈ Nominal interest rate − Inflation rate It is the price of loanable funds, and it is simultaneously the opportunity cost of holding funds, the real cost of borrowing, and the real return to lending.

The global loanable funds market

In an open economy the interest rate is set in the world market, not the domestic one. World demand (DLFW) is the sum of all countries' demands and world supply (SLFW) is the sum of all countries' supplies. The world equilibrium real interest rate makes global demand equal global supply.

International borrowerInternational lender

Small country versus large country

A small country is a price-taker: its changes do not move the world rate, only its own net borrowing or lending. A large country shifts global demand or supply enough to change the world real interest rate, affecting every other country's position.
Practice · short answer

Country A\u2019s domestic loanable funds market alone would clear at 7%. The world equilibrium real interest rate is 5%. Explain Country A\u2019s position, assuming it is a small country.

Country A becomes an international borrower.

Why: its isolated domestic rate of 7% is above the world rate of 5%, which tells us domestic investment demand is large relative to domestic saving. Once integrated into the global market, Country A faces the world rate of 5%, not 7%.

At 5%: the lower rate makes more investment projects profitable, so the quantity of loanable funds demanded rises, while the lower return discourages saving, so the quantity supplied falls. Domestic demand therefore exceeds domestic supply, and that horizontal gap is net foreign borrowing.

Consequences: funds flow in from abroad, domestic borrowers (firms and the government) get access to cheaper global capital than they could raise at home, and investment is higher than it would have been in isolation. Because Country A is small, its borrowing does not move the world rate — it simply takes the 5% as given.

Money, the Price Level and Inflation

Fast: 1.5 hSteady: 3 h Short unit, but essential. The money market diagram is the backbone of every monetary policy question.

The idea in one breath

Money pays no interest, so holding it costs you the interest you gave up. That forgone interest is the "price" of money. When it is high you hold less; when it is low you hold more. The central bank fixes how much money exists, and the interest rate moves until people are content to hold exactly that amount.

The axis is REAL money, not nominal money

Parkin plots real money = M ÷ P on the horizontal axis. This matters: a rise in the price level does not shift money demand, because people want the same real purchasing power in their wallet. Instead a higher P reduces M/P, which shifts the MS curve left. Getting this backwards is a classic error.

What moves money demand

InfluenceEffectMovement or shift?
Nominal interest rate ↑Quantity of real money demanded falls Movement along MD
Real GDP ↑More transactions, so more money neededMD shifts right
Financial innovation (cards, mobile banking)Less cash neededMD shifts left
Price level ↑Nominal money demand rises proportionally; real money demand unchanged MD does not shift

How the interest rate actually adjusts (via bonds)

Examiners like this mechanism because it shows you understand why the graph works.

  • If the interest rate is too high: holding money is expensive, so people hold less than the bank has supplied. They use the excess to buy bonds. Bond prices rise, and since a bond's fixed payment is now spread over a higher price, the interest rate falls.
  • If the interest rate is too low: people want more money than exists, so they sell bonds to get it. Bond prices fall and the interest rate rises.

Remember the inverse rule: bond price up ⟹ interest rate down. Always.

Exam-style practice · 6 marks

MS is fixed. Financial innovation shifts MD left, so at i₀ people hold more money than they want. They buy bonds, bond prices rise and the interest rate falls i₀→i₁.

Shift and reason

Financial innovation is one of the four influences on money holding. Mobile banking lets people carry out the same transactions holding less money, so at every interest rate the quantity of real money demanded falls: MD shifts left. The money supply is fixed by the central bank, so MS does not move.

New equilibrium and mechanism

At the original interest rate i₀ people now hold more money than they want. They get rid of the surplus by buying bonds. Greater demand for bonds raises bond prices, and since a bond pays a fixed sum, a higher price means a lower interest rate. This continues until the rate has fallen to i₁, where people are again content to hold exactly the money that exists. The nominal interest rate therefore falls.

Fiscal and Monetary Policy

Fast: 2 hSteady: 4 h This unit ties the other three together. A policy question is really three diagrams in a chain, so learn the chain, not isolated facts.

The idea in one breath

The government has two levers. Fiscal policy is taxing and spending. Monetary policy is controlling money and interest rates. Both end up moving the same AD curve, just by different routes.

The supply side of fiscal policy

Taxes do not only move AD; they also damage incentives, which moves LAS.

  • Tax wedge in the labour market. An income tax puts a gap between what firms pay and what workers receive. Workers decide based on the after-tax real wage, so labour supply falls, employment falls and potential GDP falls. A consumption tax widens the wedge further by reducing what a wage will buy.
  • Tax on capital income. Taxing interest income cuts the after-tax return to saving, so households save less. In the loanable funds market SLF shifts left, the real interest rate firms face rises, investment falls, capital accumulation slows and long-run growth is lower.

Monetary policy and open market operations

An open market operation is the central bank buying or selling government bonds. This is the main tool, and you must be able to describe the full sequence.

Contractionary (fighting inflation) Sell bonds → banks' reserves fall → banks lend less → deposits fall → money supply ↓ → MS shifts left → interest rate ↑ Expansionary (fighting recession) Buy bonds → new money credited to reserves → banks lend more → deposits rise → money supply ↑ → MS shifts right → interest rate ↓

Why selling bonds shrinks money

When the central bank sells a bond, the buyer pays with money that comes out of the banking system. Reserves fall. Because banks lend a multiple of their reserves, the fall in deposits is larger than the value of bonds sold. That multiplied contraction is why a modest operation moves the whole money supply.

An economy is operating above potential GDP with rising inflation. The central bank decides to act.

(a) Explain how the central bank conducts open market operations under contractionary policy, how this changes the money supply, and illustrate the effect on the nominal interest rate. (6)
(b) Explain the likely short-run effects on investment and overall economic activity. (4)

(a) Diagram

Draw Fig. 5: MD downward sloping and unchanged, MS₀ vertical shifting left to MS₁, equilibrium moving up the MD curve from E₀ to E₁, with i₁ above i₀ and M₁ left of M₀. Horizontal axis is real money (M/P).

Selling bonds drains reserves and deposits, so MS shifts left. People sell bonds to rebuild balances, bond prices fall and the nominal interest rate rises i₀→i₁.

(a) The operation and why MS shifts

To tighten, the central bank sells government bonds in the open market. Commercial banks and private investors pay for those bonds with funds drawn from the banking system, so bank reserves fall. With fewer reserves banks must cut lending, and since lending creates deposits, deposits fall by a multiple of the reserves withdrawn. The quantity of money therefore decreases and MS shifts left.

(a) New equilibrium and mechanism

At the old rate i₀ people now hold less money than they want. To rebuild their holdings they sell bonds. The extra supply of bonds pushes bond prices down, and a lower bond price for a fixed payment means a higher interest rate. Equilibrium settles at E₁, so the nominal interest rate rises from i₀ to i₁.

(b) Consequences

Investment. The higher interest rate raises the cost of borrowing. In the loanable funds market credit becomes more expensive, so projects that were marginally profitable no longer cover their financing cost and firms cut back. Investment falls. Interest-sensitive consumer spending on housing and durables falls too.

Overall activity. Lower investment and consumption mean aggregate demand shifts left. Real GDP falls back toward potential, closing the inflationary gap, and inflationary pressure eases. Employment falls and unemployment rises toward the natural rate. The intended outcome is lower inflation and stabilised prices, at the cost of slower growth in the short run.

Also examinable from Unit 07

Fiscal policy and the labour market: the tax wedge

Without taxes, the labour market clears where labour supply meets labour demand, employment is maximised and the economy produces potential GDP.

An income tax drives a tax wedge between the wage firms pay and the wage workers receive. Because workers decide how much to work based on the after-tax real wage, the incentive to work falls: labour supply contracts, employment declines, and potential GDP decreases.

A consumption tax widens the wedge

It raises the prices of goods, cutting what a wage will actually buy. The slide example: with a 25% income tax and a 10% consumption tax, $1 earned buys only $0.65 of goods.

Fiscal policy, saving and investment

A tax on capital income such as interest reduces the after-tax return to savers. Saving becomes less attractive, so households save less. In the loanable funds market SLF shifts left, the real interest rate firms face rises, and higher borrowing costs discourage investment. As investment declines, capital accumulation slows and the growth rate of real GDP falls over time.

Slide example

If taxation pushes the interest rate from 3% to 4%, investment may fall from $2 trillion to $1.8 trillion.

This is the distinction examiners hunt for

A tax cut moves both curves. It raises AD in the short run and raises potential GDP in the long run, through the labour market and the loanable funds market. Mentioning only the AD effect caps your mark.

What monetary policy is for

Monetary policy is the central bank's control of the money supply and interest rates. Its objectives are to stabilise output, control inflation, reduce unemployment and support economic growth. Unlike fiscal policy it involves no government spending or taxation.

Practice · short answer

A government cuts income tax. Explain the effect on aggregate demand AND on aggregate supply, and state why answering with only one of them loses marks.

Effect on AD (short run). A lower income tax raises households' disposable income, so consumption rises. AD shifts right, raising real GDP and employment and putting upward pressure on the price level. If the economy was already at potential, this opens an inflationary gap.

Effect on AS (long run). The tax cut narrows the tax wedge between the wage firms pay and the wage workers receive. Because workers respond to the after-tax wage, labour supply increases, employment at full employment rises, the economy moves up its production function and potential GDP rises, so LAS shifts right. If the cut also reduces tax on capital income, saving rises, SLF shifts right, the real interest rate falls and investment rises, reinforcing the LAS shift.

Why both matter. The AD effect is temporary and inflationary; the AS effect is permanent and non-inflationary. If AD and LAS both shift right, output rises with far less price pressure than the AD shift alone would cause. Answering with AD only makes the tax cut look purely inflationary and misses the entire supply-side argument, which is the point of Chapter 30. The offsetting risk to mention is the budget deficit, which raises the real interest rate and crowds out investment.

Midterm 2026 · Section B Q17.5 marks

The government of Maryland wants to boost the economy after the pandemic crisis. They have decided to reduce the income tax on wage earners from 10% to 8%. How does a decrease in income tax affect the labor market and potential GDP? Briefly discuss and depict using appropriate diagrams.

Move 1 · Draw

The question says "diagrams" in the plural, so two are required: the labour market (real wage rate against quantity of labour) and the production function (real GDP against quantity of labour).

Move 2 · Shift and why

An income tax creates a tax wedge between the wage firms pay and the wage workers receive. Workers base their supply decision on the after-tax real wage, so a tax discourages work. Cutting the rate from 10% to 8% narrows the wedge, so more people are willing to work at any given wage: labour supply shifts right, LS₀ → LS₁.
Labour demand does not shift — productivity and the marginal product of labour are unchanged. The production function does not shift either; we move along it.

Move 3 · Settle

Equilibrium moves down the unchanged labour demand curve: employment rises L₀ → L₁ while the real wage paid by firms falls slightly. Because the extra output is produced at full employment, this is a rise in potential GDP, not a temporary boom. In AD–AS terms, LAS shifts right.

Q1 — Tax-free threshold raised · 10 marks

The Finance Minister raises the tax-free income threshold for male taxpayers from Tk 2.5 lakh to Tk 3 lakh, and for female taxpayers and those over 65 from Tk 3 lakh to Tk 3.5 lakh.

(a) A reduction in tax raises after-tax income and savings. How will it change the loanable funds market? Illustrate with a labelled diagram. (4)
(b) Deficit or surplus? How does the budget condition affect the loanable funds market? Would the outcome change under Ricardo-Barro? (6)

(a) Diagram

Real interest rate on the vertical axis, loanable funds on the horizontal. DLF downward sloping and unchanged. SLF₀ shifts right to SLF₁. New equilibrium E₁ lies below and to the right of E₀. Mark r₀ above r₁, and Q₀ left of Q₁.

Higher disposable income raises saving, so SLF shifts right. The real interest rate falls r₀→r₁, the quantity of funds rises Q₀→Q₁ and investment increases.

(a) Which curve shifts and why

Raising the tax-free threshold lowers income tax paid, which raises households' disposable income. Disposable income is one of the listed shifters of the supply of loanable funds: with more income available, households save more at every real interest rate. Therefore the supply of loanable funds increases and SLF shifts right. The demand curve does not move, because neither expected profit nor the government's borrowing has changed through this channel.

(a) New equilibrium and consequences

At the old rate r₀ there is now a surplus of loanable funds. Lenders compete and the real interest rate falls to r₁. As it falls, more investment projects become profitable, so the quantity of funds demanded rises along DLF until the market clears at Q₁. The results are a lower real interest rate, a greater quantity of loanable funds and higher investment, which supports capital accumulation and long-run growth.

(b) Deficit or surplus?

Tax revenue T falls while government spending G is unchanged, so T < G. The government moves into a budget deficit (or an existing deficit widens). This is the expected outcome the question is pointing at with "serious economic implications".

(b) Effect on the loanable funds market

Diagram: DLF₀ shifts right to DLF₁. Show the private demand curve PDLF underneath, with the horizontal distance between PDLF and DLF₁ labelled as the budget deficit. New equilibrium E₁ is above and to the right of E₀.

The deficit is added to private demand PDLF, so total demand DLF₁ sits to its right. The real interest rate rises r₀→r₁ and private investment is crowded out.

Why: the government must borrow to cover the deficit. In Parkin's framework the government's borrowing is added to the private demand for loanable funds, so total demand DLF = PDLF + deficit shifts right.

Consequences: the real interest rate rises. Higher borrowing costs make some private projects unprofitable, so private investment falls. This is the crowding-out effect: government borrowing displaces private investment. Investment falls by less than the full deficit, because the higher real interest rate also induces households to save more, partly offsetting it. Lower investment means slower capital accumulation and weaker long-run growth.

(b) Combining both channels

Note that (a) and (b) push the interest rate in opposite directions: higher saving shifts SLF right (rate down) while the deficit shifts DLF right (rate up). The quantity of loanable funds unambiguously rises. The net effect on the rate depends on which shift is larger; in the standard treatment the deficit effect dominates, so the rate rises overall.

(b) Under the Ricardo-Barro effect

The outcome changes completely. Ricardo-Barro assumes taxpayers are rational and forward-looking: they understand that a deficit today must be repaid by higher taxes tomorrow, so they save the whole tax cut to meet that future liability. Private saving therefore rises by exactly the amount of the deficit, and SLF shifts right by the same distance that DLF shifted right. On the diagram both curves move right together.

Ricardo-Barro: both curves shift right by the same distance, so the real interest rate is unchanged and investment is unaffected. Only the quantity traded rises, so there is no crowding out.

The real interest rate is unchanged, investment is unchanged, and there is no crowding out; only the quantity of loanable funds traded increases. Parkin describes this as an extreme case, and most economists expect only partial adjustment in practice.

Q2 — Middle East conflict and oil prices · 10 marks

Conflict disrupts global oil supply. Crude prices rise drastically, raising production costs for almost all businesses.

(a) Using the appropriate model, explain how the rise in oil prices affects real GDP, the price level and unemployment in the short run. Illustrate. (6)
(b) Identify the type of output gap, explain why it is a problem, and discuss how the economy could return to potential GDP. (4)

(a) Diagram

Use the AD–AS model. Price level on the vertical axis, real GDP on the horizontal. Draw LAS vertical at potential GDP, SAS₀ upward sloping and AD downward sloping, all intersecting at E₀ on LAS (start at full employment). Then shift SAS₀ left to SAS₁. The new equilibrium E₁ lies up and to the left along the unchanged AD curve. Label P₀ < P₁ and Y₁ < Y₀, and mark the gap between Y₁ and potential GDP. This is exactly Fig. 2 in Unit 3 above.

SAS shifts left. E₀→E₁ climbs the unchanged AD curve, so the price level rises P₀→P₁ while real GDP falls Y₀→Y₁, opening a recessionary gap to the left of LAS.

(a) Which curve shifts and why

Oil is an input into production and transport for almost every firm in the economy. A sharp rise in the crude price raises firms' costs of production at every price level, so firms are willing to supply less real GDP at each price. Short-run aggregate supply decreases: SAS₀ → SAS₁.
AD does not shift, because nothing has changed in wealth, expectations, fiscal policy, monetary policy or the world economy through this channel.
LAS does not shift, because potential GDP is determined by the quantity of labour and capital and by technology. The oil shock is a temporary change in an input price, not a change in productive capacity.

(a) New equilibrium and consequences

Equilibrium moves from E₀ to E₁: the price level rises from P₀ to P₁ and real GDP falls from Y₀ to Y₁. Because firms are producing less output they need fewer workers, so employment falls and unemployment rises above the natural rate. Rising prices combined with falling output is stagflation, which is the distinctive signature of a negative supply shock, and it is exactly what the United States experienced in the oil crises of the 1970s and early 1980s.

(b) Type of gap and why it matters

Real GDP Y₁ is now below potential GDP, so the output gap is negative: this is a recessionary gap.
It is a problem because: output and income that could have been produced are permanently lost; unemployment above the natural rate means lost incomes for households and reduced consumption and investment; and prolonged unemployment causes lost human capital as workers' skills decay, so the damage outlives the shock itself. Meanwhile households face a higher cost of living, so real wages fall.

(b) How the economy returns to potential

Route 1 — automatic adjustment through the money wage rate. With unemployment above the natural rate there is an excess supply of labour, which puts downward pressure on the money wage rate. As wages fall, firms' costs fall, so SAS shifts back to the right toward SAS₀. Real GDP rises back to potential GDP and the price level falls back. The same happens naturally if oil prices themselves subside. This route works but is slow, because money wages are sticky downward.

Route 2 — expansionary policy. The government can raise spending or cut taxes, or the central bank can increase the money supply and lower the interest rate. Either shifts AD to the right, restoring real GDP to potential more quickly. The trade-off is that this closes the gap by raising the price level further rather than reversing the inflation, so policymakers face a genuine dilemma: fight the recession or fight the inflation, but not both at once.

Q3 — Contractionary monetary policy · 10 marks

Facing persistent inflationary pressure, the central bank of a rapidly growing economy adopts contractionary monetary policy, conducting open-market operations to reduce excess liquidity.

(a) Explain how the central bank carries out open-market operations under contractionary policy, how these actions affect the supply of money, and illustrate the impact on the nominal interest rate. (6)
(b) Explain the likely short-run effects on investment and overall economic activity. (4)

(a) Diagram

Draw the money market: nominal interest rate on the vertical axis, real money (M/P) on the horizontal. MD slopes downward. MS is vertical because the central bank fixes the quantity. Shift MS₀ left to MS₁. Equilibrium moves up along MD from E₀ to E₁, with i₁ above i₀ and M₁ to the left of M₀. This is Fig. 5 in Unit 5 above.

Selling bonds drains reserves and deposits, so MS shifts left. People sell bonds to rebuild balances, bond prices fall and the nominal interest rate rises i₀→i₁.

(a) The open market operation

An open market operation is the central bank's buying or selling of government securities. Under contractionary policy the central bank sells government bonds in the open market to commercial banks and private investors.
Buyers pay for those bonds with funds drawn out of the banking system, so banks' reserves fall. Because banks lend a multiple of their reserves, the fall in reserves forces them to reduce lending, and since bank lending creates deposits, deposits fall by a multiple of the reserves withdrawn. Deposits are the largest component of the money supply, so the quantity of money decreases and the MS curve shifts left.

(a) Why the interest rate rises

At the original rate i₀ people now hold less money than they wish to hold. To rebuild their money balances they sell bonds. This increased supply of bonds drives bond prices down, and because a bond pays a fixed sum, a lower bond price means a higher interest rate. The process continues until the rate has risen to i₁, where the quantity of real money demanded once again equals the reduced quantity supplied. The nominal interest rate rises from i₀ to i₁.

(b) Effect on investment

The higher nominal interest rate raises the real cost of borrowing. In the loanable funds market credit becomes more expensive, so projects that were only marginally profitable no longer cover their financing costs and firms abandon them. Investment falls. Interest-sensitive household spending falls too, particularly on housing, cars and other durables bought on credit, and banks also tighten lending standards so fewer borrowers qualify.

(b) Effect on overall economic activity

Lower investment and lower consumption together mean aggregate demand shifts left. In the AD–AS diagram real GDP falls back toward potential GDP, closing the inflationary gap the economy started with, and the price level rises more slowly, so inflationary pressure eases. Employment falls and unemployment rises toward the natural rate.

Overall outcome: lower inflation and more stable prices, achieved at the cost of slower growth and higher unemployment in the short run. This is the standard trade-off of contractionary monetary policy, and stating it explicitly is usually worth a mark.
